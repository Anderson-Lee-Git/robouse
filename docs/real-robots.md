# Real robots

The `real` suite runs real arms behind the same episode server and `robo` command as the simulators. By default each task runs on a hardware-in-the-loop mock that replays a session recorded on the real arm, so the whole stack runs without hardware.

```console
$ robouse run --task real-metal-reach --harness oracle --out runs/real
```

The suite has 6 tasks on the MakerMods Metal arm, the SO-101, the AgileX Piper and a ROS arm. `robo info` reports `mode: hil-mock` on the mock and `mode: real` on hardware. The mock follows commands with a tracking model fitted to the recording and shows the recorded camera frames closest to its pose. It models no contact with objects: on the mock, a pick-and-place verdict comes from a scripted stand-in operator.

`robouse robots` lists the robot profiles, drivers and recorded sessions. `robouse hil-check` replays the recorded sessions through the safety layer.

## Safety layer

The episode server checks every control step, in this order:

1. **E-stop.** `robouse estop [REASON]` latches a stop: the next command in any real episode, or the next check between requests, ends it as `safety_stop` (score 0). `robouse estop --clear` releases it.
2. **Joint limits.** Targets are clamped to the robot's limits. Where a mechanical stop has been measured, the limit sits at least 2.2 degrees inside it: on the Metal arm, elbow_flex ends at 174 (its stop is at 176.4), shoulder_lift at -2.5 (with torque off the parked arm rests at about 0) and the gripper at 2 (it closes at -0.25); on the SO-101, the gripper ends at 2 and 98 (its calibrated ends are 0 and 100) and the other joints already sat 2.5 degrees or more inside their calibrated ranges.
3. **Workspace.** On arms with a kinematic model (the Metal and the SO-101), the path to a new target is checked by forward kinematics: the arm must stay above the table and inside the workspace box. A violating move is refused before anything moves, and no step is spent.
4. **Speed.** A per-step joint speed limit, lower near the table.
5. **Wind-up.** A command never leads the measured joint by more than a few degrees.
6. **Temperature.** A motor over the limit stops the episode, also when it is still over the limit as the episode starts.
7. **Stale telemetry.** A joint reading more than 1 second old stops the episode (a cached bus keeps serving its last values when the link to the motors dies). The Piper's joints arrive in three feedback frames (joints 1-2, 3-4 and 5-6), each dated on its own: one that falls silent stops the episode, and the stop names it.
8. **Loop watchdog.** A control step slower than 2 seconds, or three in a row slower than 0.5 seconds, stops the episode. On a rig slower than about 6 control steps a second, the limits stretch to 10 and 3 control periods.
9. **Contact watch.** A move that ends within 3 cm of the table is watched: a contact stops that move, the arm holds where it is, and `robo` reports it under `contact`. A second contact, a contact anywhere else, or one during the 10 steps the arm holds still after the episode ends (`robo done`, give-up, budget or time limit) stops the episode. The Metal arm watches joint torque (thresholds from its 2026-09-05 bench sessions) and whether the fingertips stop short of a commanded descent; the SO-101 only the latter, since its driver reads no joint torque. The gripper's own torque is not watched (grasping is contact by design), but the arm joints stay watched while the gripper moves. A ROS arm watches JointState effort when its rig file sets thresholds; with no kinematic model, any contact then stops the episode.

Between requests, while the agent thinks, and while the operator answers a question, the episode server reads the arm at least once a second and checks the e-stop, the freshness of the joint readings and the temperatures; the motors keep the last command. A failed check stops the episode like a failed step.

The AgileX Piper and the bimanual SO-101 have no contact watch: their drivers read no joint torque and they have no kinematic model, so nothing detects them pushing against an object. The physical e-stop confirmation applies to them as to every live robot.

Limits and thresholds are in the robot profiles (`src/robouse/real/robots.py`); the `envelope:` and `contact:` blocks of a task or rig file override them.

**After a stop, nothing moves on its own.** After any safety stop (a contact, the e-stop, lost telemetry, the watchdog, a hot motor, a hardware fault), the command of every arm joint is brought to its measured position within the per-step speed limit, so nothing keeps pushing, and the arm holds there. The gripper keeps its command, so a grasped object is not let go. A stale reading is never used: without a fresh one, the arm keeps its last command. While the arm holds, the episode server keeps reading it once a second until the operator has answered: a motor over the stop temperature, or a link that goes quiet, is reported to the operator as a warning and written to the session log. When the server closes, an attended operator is asked to inspect the robot and whether to park the arm; only a yes moves it, once. If the arm cannot be parked (something in the gripper, no rest pose, no fresh reading), the operator is asked to acknowledge the held arm instead. After a refused gate or an interrupted run the arm holds and nothing is asked. When the link to the motors is down, the arm keeps its last command and only the physical e-stop stops it.

**When an episode ends normally.** The command of every arm joint is first brought to its measured position. If the gripper still holds something, or there is no fresh reading of the arm, the arm holds there, and that counts as a stop after the end: the result records it, `run-many` holds the batch for it, and an attended operator is asked to inspect the arm (and told to take the object out). Otherwise only a robot whose parks have a calibrated watch parks on its own, and so far that is only the Metal arm: it parks slowly at its rest pose, and torque is released once it is at rest. Every other robot (the SO-101, the bimanual SO-101, the Piper, a ROS arm) holds where it is, and an attended operator is asked whether to park it. The question says that the park is not calibrated and asks the operator to watch it: such a park stops only when an arm joint lags its command by more than 90% of the wind-up limit (5.4 degrees on the Piper, 9 on the SO-101), or, on the SO-101, when the fingertips stop short of a descent near the table. A blocked joint does reach that lag, but only after pushing for several steps with nearly the most force the wind-up limit allows. A ROS arm holds its rest pose after a park (the bridge cannot turn its motors off), and parks only at a rest pose its rig file names; a rig file that gives a calibrated lag limit and park speed (`contact: {park_lag_deg, park_speed_deg_s}`) makes its arm park on its own. A park that meets anything stops there and holds, is never retried on its own, and counts as a stop after the end.

**How the Metal arm's park is watched.** Mainly by tracking lag: an arm joint whose measured position lags its command by more than 3.3 degrees stops the park, as does, near the table, the fingertips stopping short of a commanded descent; the contact watch's torque rules, at 4x their thresholds, are the backstop. On the 2026-09-05 recordings no joint lagged by more than 2.55 degrees at the park speed (6 degrees a second, which parks never exceed) or holding, and a blocked joint's lag climbs to the 4 degree wind-up limit; on the mock, a joint held back stops the park by lag. Replayed as parks with the recorded joints and torques, the watch stops none of 56 park-length stretches of normal motion (the recorded targets as commands) and, with the commands robouse sends, catches both verified contacts of those sessions: the 12:17 table touchdown by the stall check, and the 13:16 elbow stop only by the torque backstop. Lag does not catch 13:16: robouse caps the elbow at 174 degrees (that day's harness sent it to 180, and it stopped at 176.37), so its lag against the recorded joints peaks at 2.36 degrees, under the limit. An arm following the capped command would not have reached the stop at all. The 4x factor was picked on the same 56 stretches it is scored on: 3x stops 2 of them, the first stop comes just under 3.925x (4x is 1.9% above it), and picked with the session under test left out, the factor stops 1 of the 56. A false stop only holds the arm: it costs availability, not safety.



Every intervention is written to `episode/session/events.jsonl`.

## The operator

Before a live robot is armed, the operator confirms that a physical e-stop (a power cut-off) is within reach; no setting skips this. A real episode then waits at operator gates before anything moves: `arm` (enable torque) and `reset` (the scene is set up). A refused or unanswered question stops the episode before anything moves; every question times out after `operator_timeout_s` (600 s by default) as no answer. The operator answers in the terminal that started the trial, or, with `operator: file` in the task's `real:` block, from another terminal with `robouse operator status` and `robouse operator answer yes|no`; `status` also shows how the arm was left after the last stop. `robouse operator say "..."` sends a remark the policy sees in its next `robo observe`. On the mock, a scripted operator answers and the physical e-stop is not asked.

In `robouse run-many`, a safety stop on a real robot holds the batch: the next trial waits until the operator has inspected the robot and answered `inspect`, which is recorded in the stopped trial's session log. Without an attended operator, or when the answer is no, the batch ends: `halt.json` in the run folder names the trial and the cause, and `run-many` exits with status 3. An error in the hold itself (reading the trial's result, recording the inspection, a full disk) also ends the batch with status 3, and `halt.json` gives the error when it can be written. Trials on a live robot always run one at a time, whatever `--concurrency` says.

Tasks with a measurable goal are judged from the measured joints. Tasks without one ask the operator for the verdict.
